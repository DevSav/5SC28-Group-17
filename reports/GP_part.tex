

\subsection{Method}

The GP model used the measured motor voltage and disk angle, without
angular velocity \cite{assignment5sc28}. A NARX structure was selected
because delayed inputs and outputs represent the dynamics without an
explicit state estimate.

The NARX regressor is defined as

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{x}(k) =
\big[&
u(k-n_b),\ldots,u(k-1),\\
&
\theta(k-n_a),\ldots,\theta(k-1)
\big]^{\top},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:gp_regressor}
\end{equation}

where \(n_a\) and \(n_b\) are the angle and voltage memory lengths. The
ordering matches the implementation, where \(\theta(k-1)\) is used in
the angle reconstruction \eqref{eq:gp_angle_reconstruction}.

Instead of predicting the absolute angle directly, the final model
predicts the angle increment

\begin{equation}
\Delta\theta(k)=\theta(k)-\theta(k-1).
\label{eq:gp_delta_target}
\end{equation}

The GP-NARX model is therefore written as

\begin{equation}
\Delta\theta(k)
=
f_{\mathrm{GP}}\!\left(\mathbf{x}(k)\right)+e(k),
\label{eq:gp_delta_model}
\end{equation}

and the predicted angle is reconstructed using

\begin{equation}
\hat{\theta}(k)
=
\theta(k-1)+\widehat{\Delta\theta}(k).
\label{eq:gp_angle_reconstruction}
\end{equation}

The delta-output target is smaller and smoother than the absolute angle,
so the GP models the local system evolution. This reduced recursive
simulation error compared with direct angle prediction.

A Gaussian Process defines a probability distribution over possible
nonlinear functions \cite{course5sc28}. For the model in
\eqref{eq:gp_delta_model}, the unknown function is assigned the prior

\begin{equation}
f_{\mathrm{GP}}(\mathbf{x})
\sim
\mathcal{GP}\!\left(0,k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}')\right),
\end{equation}

where \(k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}')\) is the covariance kernel. Given the
training inputs \(\mathbf{X}\) and targets \(\mathbf{y}\), conditioning
this prior on the data yields a Gaussian predictive distribution
\cite{course5sc28}. The predictive mean at a new regressor
\(\mathbf{x}_{*}\) is

\begin{equation}
\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}_{*})
=
\mathbf{k}_{*}^{\top}
\left(
\mathbf{K}+\sigma_n^2\mathbf{I}
\right)^{-1}
\mathbf{y},
\label{eq:gp_mean}
\end{equation}

while the predictive variance is

\begin{equation}
\hat{\sigma}_{f}^{2}(\mathbf{x}_{*})
=
k(\mathbf{x}_{*},\mathbf{x}_{*})
-
\mathbf{k}_{*}^{\top}
\left(
\mathbf{K}+\sigma_n^2\mathbf{I}
\right)^{-1}
\mathbf{k}_{*}.
\label{eq:gp_variance}
\end{equation}

Equations \eqref{eq:gp_mean}--\eqref{eq:gp_variance} are the full GP
predictive mean and variance \cite{course5sc28}. The mean is used as the
model output, while the variance represents uncertainty. The predictive
mean can also be written as

\begin{equation}
\hat{f}(\cdot)
=
\sum_i \hat{\alpha}_i\,k(\cdot,\mathbf{x}_i),
\qquad
\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
=
\left(\mathbf{K}+\sigma_n^2\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{y}.
\label{eq:gp_kernel_expansion}
\end{equation}

The kernel and noise hyperparameters were estimated by minimizing the
negative log marginal likelihood
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
-\log p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X},\boldsymbol{\eta})
={}& \tfrac{N}{2}\log 2\pi
+ \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{y}^{\top}\!\left(\mathbf{K}+\sigma_n^2\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\!\mathbf{y}}_{\text{data-fit term}}\\
&+ \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}\log\det\!\left(\mathbf{K}+\sigma_n^2\mathbf{I}\right)}_{\text{complexity term}}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:gp_marglik}
\end{equation}

which trades off data fit against model complexity \cite{course5sc28}.
The implementation used L-BFGS-B with analytic gradients.

Regressors and targets were normalized. After delayed-regressor
construction, there were \(24490\) training samples and \(10490\)
validation samples. The temporal order was preserved for realistic
free-run simulation.

Preliminary direct-output GP experiments compared kernels before the
final sparse delta-output GP was trained
(Table~\ref{tab:gp_kernel_comparison}).

\begin{table}[htbp]
\caption{Initial GP kernel comparison on the validation data.}
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lc}
\toprule
Kernel & Prediction RMSE \\
\midrule
RBF & 0.3583 deg \\
Mat\'ern-\(3/2\) & 0.2514 deg \\
Linear + RBF & 0.2515 deg \\
Mat\'ern-\(5/2\) & 0.2147 deg \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\label{tab:gp_kernel_comparison}
\end{table}

The Mat\'ern-\(5/2\) kernel gave the lowest validation prediction error
and was selected. It is less smooth than the RBF kernel, which is useful
for nonlinear mechanical dynamics with friction and local changes. ARD
gave each delayed input and output its own length scale.

The lag orders were then varied using

\begin{equation}
n_a\in\{5,8,10\},
\qquad
n_b\in\{5,8\}.
\label{eq:gp_lag_grid}
\end{equation}

In preliminary lag-order tests, \(n_a=10,n_b=5\) gave the best first
one-step prediction result, while \(n_a=10,n_b=8\) gave the best
free-run simulation result. Since both tasks are evaluated,
\(n_a=10,n_b=8\) was selected.

A full GP scales as \(\mathcal{O}(N^3)\), which is too expensive for
\(N=24490\) samples \cite{course5sc28}. Therefore, GPy
\texttt{SparseGPRegression} was used with \(M\ll N\) inducing points.
The inducing points were initialized by MiniBatch \(k\)-means and then
optimized together with the kernel and noise hyperparameters.

Table~\ref{tab:gp_tuning} summarizes the main modelling steps.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\caption{Main GP model tuning steps on the validation data.}
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
Model & Prediction RMSE & Simulation RMSE \\
\midrule
Direct-output GP-NARX & 0.1994 deg & 2.9658 deg \\
Delta-output GP-NARX & 0.1844 deg & 2.5333 deg \\
Sparse delta GP, \(M=150\) & 0.1698 deg & 1.5043 deg \\
Sparse delta GP, \(M=250\) & 0.1638 deg & 1.4615 deg \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\label{tab:gp_tuning}
\end{table}

Predicting the angle increment improved both errors. The sparse GP also
allowed all training samples to be used. Increasing \(M\) from 150 to
250 further reduced both errors, so \(M=250\) was selected.

The final model is consequently a sparse delta-output GP-NARX model
with a Mat\'ern-\(5/2\) ARD kernel, \(n_a=10\), \(n_b=8\), and
\(M=250\) inducing points. One-step prediction uses measured past
angles in the regressor, whereas free-run simulation recursively feeds
the previously simulated angles back into the model.

Table~\ref{tab:gp_results} summarizes the validation performance of the
final sparse delta-output GP-NARX model with \(n_a=10\), \(n_b=8\), a
Mat\'ern-\(5/2\) ARD kernel, and \(M=250\).

\begin{table}[htbp]
\caption{Validation results of the final sparse GP model.}
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
Task & RMSE [rad] & RMSE [deg] \\
\midrule
One-step prediction & 0.002859 & 0.1638 \\
Free-run simulation & 0.025508 & 1.4615 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\label{tab:gp_results}
\end{table}

Prediction error is smaller because measured past angles are used at
every sample. In simulation, only the initial output history is measured;
after that, the GP recursively uses its own simulated outputs, so errors
can accumulate.

The final simulation normalized root mean squared error was

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{NRMS}_{\mathrm{sim}} = 5.04\%.
\end{equation}

\subsection{GP Uncertainty and Optimized Hyperparameters}

Unlike a deterministic regressor, the GP also returns predictive
variance \eqref{eq:gp_variance}. The optimized hyperparameters are
summarized in Table~\ref{tab:gp_hyperparameters}.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\caption{Optimized hyperparameters of the final sparse GP model
(normalized \(\Delta\theta\) output units).}
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lc}
\toprule
Hyperparameter & Value \\
\midrule
Kernel variance \(\sigma_f^2\)   & \(0.674\) \\
Noise variance \(\sigma_n^2\)    & \(5.15\times 10^{-4}\) \\
ARD length scales                & \(18\) (one per regressor) \\
Inducing points \(M\)            & \(250\) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\label{tab:gp_hyperparameters}
\end{table}

The \(18\) ARD length scales correspond to \(n_a+n_b=18\), one for each
delayed angle and voltage sample. The small noise variance indicates a
high signal-to-noise fit, consistent with the small prediction error.

This variance is one-step uncertainty for a deterministic input. In
free-run simulation the regressor contains uncertain simulated angles,
but the implementation propagates only the predictive mean. Therefore,
the confidence bound underestimates true simulation uncertainty.


\subsection{GP Discussion}

The final sparse delta-output GP-NARX model gave a prediction RMSE of
\(0.1638\) deg and a simulation RMSE of \(1.4615\) deg. The larger
simulation error is expected because simulation recursively feeds back
the model's own outputs. The main limitations are the finite search over
kernels, lag orders, and inducing-point counts, and the fact that a GP is
most reliable in regions covered by the training data. In free-run
simulation only the mean was propagated, so uncertainty was not fully
propagated through time.

Finally, the true measured outputs for the hidden benchmark tests were
not available during development. Consequently, the final hidden
prediction and simulation files could only be checked for the required
format and dimensions before submission. Their actual performance will
be evaluated by the course staff.
